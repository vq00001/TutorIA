This chapter reviews the two modelling frameworks developed in the first part
of the course. The first is \emph{Gaussian process regression}, which models an
unknown functional relationship by placing a prior distribution over functions
instead of committing to a parametric form. The second is \emph{inference in
Bayesian networks}, which answers queries about a joint distribution by Bayes'
theorem and marginalization over the variables that are not of interest. The
two are complementary: the Gaussian process supplies a flexible prior and
likelihood for a regression problem, and the network manipulations turn a joint
model into posterior statements about individual variables. The review revisits
the definition of a Gaussian process, the Bayesian route from prior and
likelihood to posterior and predictive distributions, and the learning of
kernel hyperparameters; it then recalls the product rule, states Bayes' theorem
with background conditioning, and works through the fuel-gauge example, in
which an observed gauge reading changes the probability that the fuel tank is
empty and the state of the battery then explains the reading away.

\section{Gaussian Process Regression}
\label{sec:rev-gp}

Regression learns a mapping from an input $\mathbf{x}$ to a real-valued target
$y$ from a set of examples. A parametric model such as linear regression
commits in advance to a functional form; when the relationship between inputs
and targets is not apparent from the data, a Gaussian process instead places a
distribution over the unknown function itself. The prior encodes only
qualitative knowledge---smoothness, length scale, amplitude---and the data then
select the functions that are consistent with it. This section reviews the
prior over functions, the posterior over the training values, the predictive
distribution at a new input, and the learning of the kernel parameters from
data.

\subsection{The prior over functions}

A Gaussian process is a Gaussian process is a
collection of random variables $f(\mathbf{x})$ indexed by the input
$\mathbf{x}$ such that the values at any finite set of inputs are jointly
Gaussian, with the specification consistent across finite sets. The process is
determined by its mean function $m(\mathbf{x})=\mathbb{E}[f(\mathbf{x})]$ and
its covariance function
$k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')
 =\mathbb{E}[(f(\mathbf{x})-m(\mathbf{x}))
          (f(\mathbf{x}')-m(\mathbf{x}'))]$,
and one writes $f\sim\mathcal{GP}(m,k)$. Following
the convention of the usual zero-mean convention, the mean function is taken to be
zero, $m(\mathbf{x})\equiv0$, so that the process is fixed by the kernel alone:
\begin{equation}
  f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k).
  \label{rev:eq-gp-prior}
\end{equation}
The kernel is the object that models the relationship: inputs that are similar
under the kernel receive strongly correlated function values. The
squared-exponential kernel
$k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}')
 =\sigma_f^2\exp(-\lVert\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'\rVert^{2}/2\ell^{2})$
is the standard choice for smooth functions, with the signal variance
$\sigma_f^2$ and the length scale $\ell$ controlling the vertical and
horizontal scales of the prior.

For training inputs $\mathbf{X}=(\mathbf{x}_1,\ldots,\mathbf{x}_n)$ collect the
function values in
$\mathbf{f}=(f(\mathbf{x}_1),\ldots,f(\mathbf{x}_n))^{\mathsf{T}}$. By the
definition, $\mathbf{f}$ is Gaussian; under the zero-mean prior its
distribution is
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf{f}\mid\mathbf{X})
  =\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{f}\mid\mathbf{0},\mathbf{K}\bigr),
  \qquad
  K_{ij}=k(\mathbf{x}_i,\mathbf{x}_j),
  \label{rev:eq-gp-training-prior}
\end{equation}
that is, the Gram matrix $\mathbf{K}$ of the training inputs is the covariance
\cite[Eq.~(6.60), Sec.~6.4.2, p.~306]{bishop2006prml}. More generally, for any
two lists of inputs $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$ write
$\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B})$ for the matrix with entries
$k(\mathbf{a}_i,\mathbf{b}_j)$. The training
values and the values
$\mathbf{f}_*=(f(\mathbf{x}_{*1}),\ldots,f(\mathbf{x}_{*m}))^{\mathsf{T}}$ at
test inputs are then jointly Gaussian under the prior,
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{f}\\ \mathbf{f}_*\end{pmatrix}
  \sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(
    \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\;
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}) & \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}_*)\\
      \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}_*,\mathbf{X}) & \mathbf{K}(\mathbf{X}_*,\mathbf{X}_*)
    \end{pmatrix}\right).
  \label{rev:eq-gp-prior-joint}
\end{equation}
The block matrix specifies the Gaussian process at the combined input set: the
diagonal blocks are the Gram matrices of the training and of the test inputs,
and the off-diagonal blocks collect the covariances between the two sets
\cite[Eq.~(6.64), Sec.~6.4.2, p.~307]{bishop2006prml}.

\subsection{Posterior and predictive distributions}

In a regression problem the latent function values are not observed; the
targets are noisy measurements
\begin{equation}
  y_i=f(\mathbf{x}_i)+\varepsilon_i,
  \qquad
  \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{0},
    \sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}_n\bigr),
  \label{rev:eq-gp-noise}
\end{equation}
so the likelihood of the latent vector $\mathbf{f}$ is
$\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{f},\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}_n)$. Bayes' theorem turns the prior
and the likelihood into the posterior distribution of the function values,
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf{f}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
  \;\propto\;
  p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{f})\,p(\mathbf{f}\mid\mathbf{X}),
  \label{rev:eq-gp-posterior-propto}
\end{equation}
the function-space reading of the general update: posterior proportional to
likelihood times prior.

\begin{studybox}[title={Supplementary derivation: posterior and prediction}]
\begin{proposition}[Posterior over the training function values]
\label[proposition]{rev:prop-training-posterior}
Let $f\sim\mathcal{GP}(0,k)$ with Gram matrix $\mathbf{K}$ positive definite,
and let $\mathbf{y}=\mathbf{f}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with
$\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}_n)$
independent of $\mathbf{f}$. Write
$\mathbf{C}_n=\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}_n$. Then
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf{f}\mid\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
  =\mathcal{N}\Bigl(\mathbf{f}\,\Bigm|\,
     \mathbf{K}\mathbf{C}_n^{-1}\mathbf{y},\;
     \mathbf{K}-\mathbf{K}\mathbf{C}_n^{-1}\mathbf{K}\Bigr).
  \label{rev:eq-gp-training-posterior}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The pair $(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{y})$ is a linear transformation of the jointly
Gaussian vector $(\mathbf{f},\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})$, hence jointly Gaussian
with zero mean and covariance blocks
$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{f})=\mathbf{K}$,
$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{y})
 =\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{f}(\mathbf{f}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon})^{\mathsf{T}}]
 =\mathbf{K}$ by independence of the noise, and
$\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf{y})
 =\mathbf{K}+\sigma_n^2\mathbf{I}_n=\mathbf{C}_n$, so that
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{f}\\ \mathbf{y}\end{pmatrix}
  \sim\mathcal{N}\!\left(
    \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{0}\\ \mathbf{0}\end{pmatrix},\;
    \begin{pmatrix}
      \mathbf{K} & \mathbf{K}\\
      \mathbf{K} & \mathbf{C}_n
    \end{pmatrix}\right).
  \label{rev:eq-gp-joint-fy}
\end{equation}
The covariance is positive definite because $\mathbf{C}_n$ is, and $\mathbf{K}$ is positive definite by
assumption. Applying the conditional-Gaussian formula with
$\mathbf{z}_a=\mathbf{f}$, $\mathbf{z}_b=\mathbf{y}$,
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{aa}=\mathbf{K}$,
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{ab}=\mathbf{K}$, and
$\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{bb}=\mathbf{C}_n$ gives
\cref{rev:eq-gp-training-posterior}.
\end{proof}

The posterior mean $\mathbf{K}\mathbf{C}_n^{-1}\mathbf{y}$ is a smoothed
version of the observations, and the posterior covariance is the prior
covariance reduced by the information the data carry; by the Woodbury identity it also equals
$(\mathbf{K}^{-1}+\sigma_n^{-2}\mathbf{I}_n)^{-1}$, which shows the reduction
explicitly.

Prediction at a test input $\mathbf{x}_*$ asks for the distribution of
$f_*=f(\mathbf{x}_*)$ given the data. The pair $(\mathbf{y},f_*)$ is jointly
Gaussian with covariance
\begin{equation}
  \begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{C}_n & \mathbf{k}_*\\
                  \mathbf{k}_*^{\mathsf{T}} & c\end{pmatrix},
  \qquad
  \mathbf{k}_*=\bigl(k(\mathbf{x}_1,\mathbf{x}_*),\ldots,
                    k(\mathbf{x}_n,\mathbf{x}_*)\bigr)^{\mathsf{T}},
  \qquad
  c=k(\mathbf{x}_*,\mathbf{x}_*),
  \label{rev:eq-gp-predictive-joint}
\end{equation}
as in the joint noisy GP distribution, so conditioning once more with
the conditional-Gaussian formula gives the predictive distribution
\begin{equation}
  p(f_*\mid\mathbf{x}_*,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{y})
  =\mathcal{N}\Bigl(f_*\,\Bigm|\,
     \mathbf{k}_*^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}_n^{-1}\mathbf{y},\;
     c-\mathbf{k}_*^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}_n^{-1}\mathbf{k}_*\Bigr),
  \label{rev:eq-gp-predictive}
\end{equation}
which is the noisy GP predictive result.
The noiseless counterpart uses the noiseless counterpart, in which
$\mathbf{K}$ replaces $\mathbf{C}_n$ and the observed function values replace
the targets. The predictive mean is a linear combination of the observations
with weights $\mathbf{C}_n^{-1}\mathbf{k}_*$, and the predictive variance is
small near the training inputs and reverts to the prior variance $c$ away from
them (the usual kernel-expansion interpretation).

\end{studybox}

The notes also emphasize a practical diagnostic: if the posterior uncertainty
is large relative to the scale of the observed targets, the fit remains weak
at those inputs, even when the posterior mean looks smooth.

\subsection{Learning the hyperparameters}
\begin{studybox}[title={Supplementary answer: learning kernel hyperparameters}]

The kernel is usually taken from a parametric family, and the question is
whether its parameters can be tuned to the data rather than fixed in advance.
The parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$---the signal variance $\sigma_f^2$ and
length scale $\ell$ of the squared-exponential kernel, together with the noise
variance $\sigma_n^2$---are hyperparameters of the model. They can be learned by maximizing the
\emph{marginal likelihood}, or evidence, obtained by integrating the latent
values out of the likelihood:
\begin{equation}
  p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})
  =\int p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{f})\,p(\mathbf{f}\mid\mathbf{X},
    \boldsymbol{\theta})\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{f}
  =\mathcal{N}\bigl(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{0},
    \mathbf{C}_n(\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr),
  \label{rev:eq-gp-evidence}
\end{equation}
which for the Gaussian model is available in closed form. Taking logarithms
gives
\begin{equation}
  \ln p(\mathbf{y}\mid\mathbf{X},\boldsymbol{\theta})
  =-\tfrac12\ln\lvert\mathbf{C}_n\rvert
   -\tfrac12\mathbf{y}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{C}_n^{-1}\mathbf{y}
   -\tfrac{n}{2}\ln(2\pi),
  \label{rev:eq-gp-log-evidence}
\end{equation}
a sum of a complexity penalty that rewards small covariance volume and a data
fit term that rewards agreement with the observations
\cite[Eqs.~(6.69)--(6.70), Sec.~6.4.3, pp.~311--312]{bishop2006prml}. The
function is in general nonconvex, so one resorts to gradient-based
optimization, and the fitted hyperparameters are then used in the posterior
\cref{rev:eq-gp-training-posterior} and the predictive distribution
\cref{rev:eq-gp-predictive}.

\end{studybox}

\section{Bayesian Networks and Bayes' Theorem}
\label{sec:rev-bn}

A Bayesian network compresses a joint distribution over variables
$x_1,\ldots,x_K$ by reading their dependencies off a directed acyclic graph:
the joint factorizes into one conditional distribution per node given its
parents, $p(x_1,\ldots,x_K)=\prod_{k}p(x_k\mid\pa(x_k))$. Inference then manipulates this factorization
with two elementary rules.

\begin{proposition}[Conditioned Bayes' theorem]
\label[proposition]{rev:prop-conditioned-bayes}
For events $A$, $B$, and $C$ with $p(B,C)>0$,
\begin{equation}
  p(A,B)=p(A)\,p(B\mid A)=p(B)\,p(A\mid B),
  \label{rev:eq-product-rule}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
  p(A\mid B,C)=\frac{p(B\mid A,C)\,p(A\mid C)}{p(B\mid C)}.
  \label{rev:eq-bayes-conditioned}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
The two orders are the product rule written in opposite directions. For the
conditioned form, the definition of conditional probability
(the definition of conditional probability) gives
$p(A\mid B,C)=p(A,B,C)/p(B,C)$; applying the product rule in the order $C$
first, then $A$, then $B$, yields
$p(A,B,C)=p(B\mid A,C)\,p(A\mid C)\,p(C)$ and
$p(B,C)=p(B\mid C)\,p(C)$, and cancelling the common factor $p(C)$ gives
\cref{rev:eq-bayes-conditioned}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Equivalent rearrangements]
\color{blue!70!black}
\label[remark]{rev:rem-bayes-rearrangements}
Dividing the numerator and the denominator of
\cref{rev:eq-bayes-conditioned} by $p(C)$ instead of inserting it first gives
\begin{equation}
  p(A\mid B,C)=\frac{p(A,B\mid C)}{p(B\mid C)},
  \label{rev:eq-bayes-joint-form}
\end{equation}
and the same product-rule rearrangement with the roles of the two conditioning
events exchanged gives the symmetric form
\begin{equation}
  p(A\mid B,C)=\frac{p(A,C\mid B)}{p(C\mid B)}.
  \label{rev:eq-bayes-symmetric}
\end{equation}
All the forms are the same posterior; the one to use in a computation is the
one whose conditional probabilities are read directly from the model.
\end{remark}

\begin{example}[The car battery and the fuel gauge]
\label[example]{rev:exa-fuel-gauge}
\begin{figure}[htbp]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[>=Stealth,
      revword/.style={circle,draw,inner sep=2pt,font=\small}]
    \node[revword] (b) at (0,2.4) {$B$};
    \node[revword] (f) at (0,0)   {$F$};
    \node[revword] (g) at (3.6,1.2) {$G$};
    \draw[->] (b) -- (g);
    \draw[->] (f) -- (g);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{The fuel system of a car: the battery state $B$ and the fuel-tank
  state $F$ are independent causes of the reading $G$ of the electric fuel
  gauge. The gauge is the common effect, or collider, on the trail joining the
  two causes.}
  \label{fig:rev-fuel}
\end{figure}

\begin{issuebox}[title={Source reconciliation for the numerical example}]
Two source files use conflicting readings of the prior or of one conditional
probability. The calculation below uses $p(B=1)=p(F=1)=0.9$ and
$p(G=1\mid B=0,F=1)=0.2$, the specification shared by the coherent majority
of the notes and the one that yields the recorded results $0.257$ and $0.111$.
The alternative readings are documented in the accompanying integration report.
\end{issuebox}

A car has a battery that is either charged ($B=1$) or flat ($B=0$), a fuel
tank that is either full ($F=1$) or empty ($F=0$), and an electric fuel gauge
that reads either full ($G=1$) or empty ($G=0$). The battery and the tank are
independent causes of the gauge reading, as in \cref{fig:rev-fuel}, with prior
probabilities
\begin{equation}
  p(B=1)=0.9,
  \qquad
  p(F=1)=0.9,
  \label{rev:eq-fuel-priors}
\end{equation}
and the gauge is rather unreliable, reading full with conditional
probabilities
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    p(G=1\mid B=1,F=1)&=0.8, &
    p(G=1\mid B=1,F=0)&=0.2,\\
    p(G=1\mid B=0,F=1)&=0.2, &
    p(G=1\mid B=0,F=0)&=0.1.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{rev:eq-fuel-cpds}
\end{equation}
Each row of the conditional table sums to one, so that the factorization of
the Bayesian-network factorization principle,
\begin{equation}
  p(B,F,G)=p(B)\,p(F)\,p(G\mid B,F),
  \label{rev:eq-fuel-factorization}
\end{equation}
is a complete specification of the model, and the graph encodes that $B$ and
$F$ are marginally independent:
$p(F=0\mid B=0)=p(F=0)=0.1$.

Suppose the driver turns on the ignition and the gauge reads empty, $G=0$. The
probability of the reading is obtained from the law of total probability
(the law of total probability) by marginalizing over the two unobserved
causes:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    p(G=0)
    &=\sum_{B\in\{0,1\}}\sum_{F\in\{0,1\}}
      p(G=0\mid B,F)\,p(B)\,p(F)\\
    &=0.9\cdot0.1\cdot0.1+0.8\cdot0.1\cdot0.9
      +0.8\cdot0.9\cdot0.1+0.2\cdot0.9\cdot0.9
    =0.315.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{rev:eq-fuel-evidence}
\end{equation}
The likelihood of the reading under an empty tank marginalizes over the
battery,
\begin{equation}
  p(G=0\mid F=0)
  =\sum_{B\in\{0,1\}}p(G=0\mid B,F=0)\,p(B)
  =0.9\cdot0.1+0.8\cdot0.9=0.81,
  \label{rev:eq-fuel-likelihood}
\end{equation}
and Bayes' theorem updates the prior $p(F=0)=0.1$ to
\begin{equation}
  p(F=0\mid G=0)
  =\frac{p(G=0\mid F=0)\,p(F=0)}{p(G=0)}
  =\frac{0.81\cdot0.1}{0.315}
  \simeq0.257.
  \label{rev:eq-fuel-posterior}
\end{equation}
The empty reading thus raises the probability that the tank is empty from
$0.1$ to about $0.257$, as expected.

Now suppose the driver also checks the battery and finds it flat, $B=0$.
Because $B$ and $F$ are independent causes,
$p(F=0\mid B=0)=p(F=0)$, and the prior $p(B=0)$ cancels between numerator and
denominator in Bayes' theorem, leaving
\begin{equation}
  \begin{aligned}
    p(F=0\mid G=0,B=0)
    &=\frac{p(G=0\mid B=0,F=0)\,p(F=0)}
            {\sum_{F\in\{0,1\}}p(G=0\mid B=0,F)\,p(F)}\\
    &=\frac{0.9\cdot0.1}{0.8\cdot0.9+0.9\cdot0.1}
     =\frac{0.09}{0.81}
     \simeq0.111.
  \end{aligned}
  \label{rev:eq-fuel-explained}
\end{equation}
The probability that the tank is empty has dropped from $0.257$ to about
$0.111$. The flat battery provides an alternative explanation for the empty
reading, so it \emph{explains away} the evidence that the gauge supplied in
favour of an empty tank: the two causes, which are marginally independent,
become dependent once their common effect is observed, which is the defining collider effect. This is the standard numerical form of explaining away; observing a descendant of the gauge instead of
the gauge itself would have the same effect (the descendant-of-a-collider case).
Note finally that $p(F=0\mid G=0,B=0)\simeq0.111$ still exceeds the prior
$p(F=0)=0.1$, because the empty reading remains some evidence in favour of an
empty tank even after the battery is accounted for. This pair of updates is
the whole of the inference step: the factorization
\cref{rev:eq-fuel-factorization} supplies the numbers, and marginalization and
Bayes' theorem convert them into posterior statements about any variable of
interest.
\end{example}
