This chapter reviews the two modelling frameworks developed in the first part of the course. The
first is Gaussian process regression, which models an unknown functional relationship by placing
a prior distribution over functions instead of committing to a parametric form. The second is
inference in Bayesian networks, which answers queries about a joint distribution by Bayes’ theorem
and marginalization over the variables that are not of interest. The two are complementary: the
Gaussian process supplies a flexible prior and likelihood for a regression problem, and the network
manipulations turn a joint model into posterior statements about individual variables. The review
revisits the definition of a Gaussian process, the Bayesian route from prior and likelihood to posterior
and predictive distributions, and the learning of kernel hyperparameters; it then recalls the product
rule, states Bayes’ theorem with background conditioning, and works through the fuel-gauge example,
in which an observed gauge reading changes the probability that the fuel tank is empty and the
state of the battery then explains the reading away.
1 Gaussian Process Regression
Regression learns a mapping from an input x to a real-valued target y from a set of examples.
A parametric model such as linear regression commits in advance to a functional form; when
the relationship between inputs and targets is not apparent from the data, a Gaussian process
instead places a distribution over the unknown function itself. The prior encodes only qualitative
knowledge—smoothness, length scale, amplitude—and the data then select the functions that are
consistent with it. This section reviews the prior over functions, the posterior over the training
values, the predictive distribution at a new input, and the learning of the kernel parameters from
data.

2 Bayesian Networks and Bayes’ Theorem
A Bayesian network compresses a joint distribution over variables x1, . . . , xK by reading their
dependencies off a directed acyclic graph: the joint factorizes into one conditional distribution
per node given its parents, p(x1, . . . , xK) = Q
k
p(xk | pa(xk)). Inference then manipulates this
factorization with two elementary rules.
Proposition 2.1 (Conditioned Bayes’ theorem). For events A, B, and C with p(B, C) > 0,
p(A, B) = p(A) p(B | A) = p(B) p(A | B), (12)
and
p(A | B, C) = p(B | A, C) p(A | C)
p(B | C)
. (13)
Proof. The two orders are the product rule written in opposite directions. For the conditioned
form, the definition of conditional probability (the definition of conditional probability) gives
p(A | B, C) = p(A, B, C)/p(B, C); applying the product rule in the order C first, then A, then
B, yields p(A, B, C) = p(B | A, C) p(A | C) p(C) and p(B, C) = p(B | C) p(C), and cancelling the
common factor p(C) gives equation (13).

of updates is the whole of the inference step: the factorization equation (18) supplies the numbers,
and marginalization and Bayes’ theorem convert them into posterior statements about any variable
of interest.
